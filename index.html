H₀ is on trial.

In classical statistics you are the judge. The null hypothesis is the defendant, presumed innocent. Your data are the evidence. Four ideas decide every case: the p-value, α, the mistakes a judge can make, and the confidence interval.

Case file: Atorvastatin vs Simvastatin
The defendant
H₀: “Both statins reduce cholesterol by the same amount.” (mean difference = 0)
The evidence
Atorvastatin (n = 50): mean reduction 45 mg/dL, SD 8.
Simvastatin (n = 50): mean reduction 38 mg/dL, SD 6.
Presumption
H₀ is innocent until the evidence becomes too embarrassing to explain by chance.
Possible verdicts
Guilty: reject H₀. Not guilty: fail to reject H₀.
There is no verdict called “innocent.” We never prove H₀ true.

Exhibit A

The p-value: how embarrassing is the evidence?

Pretend H₀ is true: the two statins are identical. The p-value asks one question: if that were true, how often would chance alone produce a difference at least as extreme as the one we saw?

“A p value is a measure of how embarrassing the data are to the null hypothesis.”Nicholas Maxwell, Data Matters (2004)

  • Bigger difference → further into the tail → smaller p.
  • More patients → smaller p for the same difference. Try a 0.5 mg/dL difference with 5,000 per group: “significant,” yet clinically meaningless.
  • p is computed from your data. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, and a large p only means “get more data.”
Exhibit B

α: the bar you set before the trial starts

Before any patient is enrolled, the court decides how much risk of convicting an innocent H₀ it will tolerate. That pre-set risk is α. With α = 0.05 it is split into 0.025 in each tail, which for 98 degrees of freedom puts the critical values at t = ±1.98.

Decision rule (Neyman–Pearson): if p ≤ α, reject H₀. Fisher preferred simply reporting the p-value as a continuous measure of evidence, without the rigid yes/no cut.

α controls how strict the test is. β is the chance of missing a real effect. Power = 1 − β, and studies are usually designed for power of at least 80% (β = 0.20). Now watch them fight:

Try it: make α stricter (0.01) and β grows. The only way to shrink both is more patients or a bigger true effect. (Normal approximation, two-sided test.)

Put 20 useless drugs on trial

Each drug below does nothing. Test each at α = 0.05 and, on average, one will be “significant” purely by chance. That is a Type I error, also called a false discovery.

The more questions you ask, the more chances the data get to look extreme. Bonferroni fixes it by being very strict (and loses power); leaving p-values uncorrected is too lenient. There is no universal recipe.

Exhibit E

Confidence intervals: the promise is about the net

Instead of a guilty/not guilty verdict, ask two questions. What is the single best guess for the true difference? That is the point estimate. What range of differences is plausible? That is the confidence interval.

  • 95% describes the method: in the long run, 95% of intervals built this way catch the truth.
  • Any single interval either contains the true value or it doesn’t (probability 1 or 0). We just don’t know which.
  • If α = 0.05, the matching interval is 95%. A wide interval means more uncertainty.
Exhibit F

Read the interval, not just the p-value

A CI shows the size of the effect and its uncertainty, which a p-value never does. Make your call on each case, then reveal.

Statins

+7.0 mg/dL (95% CI 4.2 to 9.8), p < 0.001
The whole interval sits above 0, so it’s statistically significant and consistent with a real advantage for atorvastatin. Whether 4 to 10 mg/dL matters to patients is a clinical question, not a statistical one.

BP drug in a huge trial

−0.5 mmHg (95% CI −0.7 to −0.3), p < 0.0001
Tiny p, tiny effect. The interval is narrow because n is enormous, but half a millimetre of mercury changes nothing for a patient. Statistically significant, clinically meaningless. (Interval drawn for illustration.)

Aspirin for secondary stroke

Risk difference +2% (95% CI −3% to +7%)
The interval crosses 0 and includes both benefit and harm, so the result is inconclusive. It does not prove aspirin has no effect; not guilty ≠ innocent.

The whole lecture on one screen

p-value
If H₀ were true, the probability of data at least as extreme as ours. Computed from the data. Small p = embarrassing for H₀.
α
The pre-set risk of a false alarm we accept (usually 0.05). Decided before the data. Reject H₀ if p ≤ α.
Type I error
Rejecting a true H₀: convicting the innocent. Probability = α.
Type II error
Failing to reject a false H₀: the guilty walks free. Probability = β. Power = 1 − β, usually targeted at ≥ 80%.
Confidence interval
Point estimate ± a margin. 95% of intervals built this way catch the truth in the long run. Shows effect size and uncertainty.

Things we don’t say in this class

“p = 0.03 means a 3% chance H₀ is true.”
It assumes H₀ is true; it can’t measure it.
“p > 0.05, so there’s no effect.”
Absence of evidence isn’t evidence of absence.
“There’s a 95% chance the truth is in this interval.”
The 95% belongs to the method, not to one interval.
“p < 0.001, so the effect is huge.”
p says nothing about size. Look at the CI.
